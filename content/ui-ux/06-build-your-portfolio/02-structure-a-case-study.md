# Structure a case study

*A case study is a story with a beginning, a middle and an end: the problem, what you did, and what happened.*

## TL;DR

A case study is a story with a beginning, a middle and an end: the problem, what you did, and what happened.

---

## Chapter 1: The trip story

When you tell friends about a trip, you don't go hour by hour. You tell them the point, the surprise and the lesson. "We missed the train, ended up in a tiny town, and it was the best day."

A case study is the same. The reader doesn't need every step. They need the story of what you learned and decided.

**In real life:** A gallery of screens with no explanation is a photo album with no captions. Nice to look at. Impossible to understand.

---

## Chapter 2: The skeleton

This structure works for most UX projects. Use what fits yours.

| Section | It answers | It shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Summary | What is this, and what was my role? | Project, role, timeline, tools, one-line outcome |
| 2. Problem | Who has a problem, and why does it matter? | You understand the situation |
| 3. Research | What did I learn, and how do I know? | Evidence, quotes, patterns |
| 4. Define | What exactly are we solving? | Problem statement and HMW |
| 5. Explore | What ideas did I consider? | Sketches, flows, choices |
| 6. Design | What did I make, and why? | Wireframes to final screens, key decisions |
| 7. Test | Did it work? | Test findings and what changed |
| 8. Outcome | What happened, and what did I learn? | Results, reflection, next steps |

---

## Chapter 3: Show decisions, not just screens

Anyone can show a screen. What makes your thinking visible is why it looks that way.

For each important decision, write down four things: the options you had, what you chose, why you chose it, and the evidence. Here's an invented example of the shape:

> "I considered a menu icon and a bottom tab bar. I chose the tab bar because in testing, three of five people couldn't find the menu icon."

That's the style. Use your own real findings, never invented ones.

---

## Chapter 4: Length and flow

1. **Lead with the best.** Put the summary and the strongest result near the top.
2. **Make it skimmable.** Headings, short paragraphs and one image per point.
3. **Give people two speeds.** A quick version at the top, with more detail for those who keep reading.
4. **Cut what doesn't earn its place.** If a section doesn't change the story, trim it.
5. **End with a reflection.** What you learned and what you'd do next.

---

## Common mistakes

- A gallery of screens with no story
- Starting with only final screens
- Dumping all your process with no decisions
- Skipping the outcome and the reflection

---

## Try it (25 min)

- [ ] Write the eight section names on a page.
- [ ] Under each, write one sentence about your project.
- [ ] Mark the sections where you have evidence, like quotes, sketches or test notes.
- [ ] Note the sections where you're missing evidence, and decide what to do.
