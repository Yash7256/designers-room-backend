# Who are you designing for?

*"Everyone" is not a user. Pick a specific person, work out what they're trying to do, and design for them.*

## TL;DR

"Everyone" is not a user. Pick a specific person, work out what they're trying to do, and design for them.

---

## Chapter 1: Designing for everyone is designing for nobody

Imagine you're buying a birthday gift. You don't think about "people". You think about one person: your cousin who loves cricket and hates clutter. That's why the gift ends up good.

Design works the same way. An app "for everyone" ends up feeling just okay for nobody. A sharp idea for one specific person often turns out useful for many more.

**In real life:** A college canteen that tries to please everyone: pizza, dosa, sandwiches, noodles and a thali, all on one menu. Nothing is great. The little stall that makes one thing really well has a queue.

---

## Chapter 2: Users, buyers and the people nearby

The person using something isn't always the only person who matters.

- **User:** The person who actually uses it. Your main focus.
- **Buyer:** The person who pays or decides. Sometimes the same person, sometimes not. In a school app, students use it, but parents might pay.
- **Stakeholder:** Anyone affected by it or involved in making it work: your team, a manager, teachers, support staff.
- **Edge-case user:** Someone at the fringes: using one hand, on slow internet, with low vision, in bright sunlight. Designing for them tends to improve things for everyone.

---

## Chapter 3: Describe your person in four questions

Forget "Age 18 to 35, lives in a city". That tells you almost nothing. Describe a situation instead.

1. **Who are they, in context?** "A second-year student who shares a hostel room and studies late on slow Wi-Fi" is far more useful than "a student".
2. **What are they trying to do?** The goal, not the feature.
3. **What do they do today?** Including workarounds. If they're already doing something clumsy to solve this, that's gold.
4. **What gets in their way?** The frustration, delay or worry that makes it hard.

---

## Chapter 4: Don't guess, go and look

Here's the uncomfortable truth: right now, everything you believe about your user is a guess. Guesses are fine to start with, as long as you know they're guesses.

Write them down as **"We believe that..."** statements. For example: "We believe first-year students lose notes before exams." Then pick the riskiest one, the one that would sink the idea if you're wrong, and test it first. The next pages show you how.

---

## Common mistakes

- Choosing "everyone aged 18 to 60"
- Designing for yourself and assuming you're typical
- Only imagining the easy, happy-path user
- Treating assumptions as facts

- [ ] Write three sentences describing one specific person who'd use it.
- [ ] List five "We believe that..." assumptions.
- [ ] Circle the riskiest one.
