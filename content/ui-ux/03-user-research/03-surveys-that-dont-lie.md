# Surveys that don't lie

*Surveys tell you what and how many. Interviews tell you why. Write questions that don't push people toward an answer.*

## TL;DR

Surveys tell you what and how many. Interviews tell you why. Write questions that don't push people toward an answer.

---

## Chapter 1: What a survey is good for

A poll in the group chat, "Which movie tonight?", gets answers in minutes. But you only learn what people picked, not why.

Surveys are great for checking whether something you've spotted is common, or for comparing options. They're poor at discovering problems you don't know about yet, and bad at explaining reasons. So the usual order is interviews first, survey after.

**In real life:** A college fest feedback form that says "Rate our event from 1 to 5." You learn the average was 3.8. You don't learn that the sound system was the problem.

---

## Chapter 2: Four ways questions lie

- **Leading:** "How great was our checkout?" assumes it was great. Better: "How was your experience with checkout?"
- **Loaded:** "When did you last enjoy using our app?" assumes they enjoyed it. Better: "How would you describe using our app?"
- **Double-barreled:** "Is the app fast and easy to use?" is two questions. Someone might say yes to one and no to the other. Split it.
- **Vague:** "Do you use it often?" What does "often" mean? Better: "How many days in the last week did you use it?"

---

## Chapter 3: Write a survey people finish

1. **Start with a decision.** What will you do differently depending on the answers? If nothing, skip the question.
2. **Keep it short.** A few minutes at most. Every question must earn its place.
3. **Mix question types.** Mostly choice questions, plus one or two open boxes for "anything else?".
4. **Label your scales.** "1 is very hard, 5 is very easy" beats unlabeled numbers.
5. **Ask about behavior, not predictions.** "How many times did you order last week?" beats "How often will you order?".
6. **Test it on two friends first.** If either hesitates at a question, rewrite it.

---

## Chapter 4: Read the results honestly

The people who answer surveys are often the ones with strong opinions, and a small group can't speak for everyone. So be careful with your words.

If 12 people answered, say "12 people answered", not "most users agree". Share the counts. Say what you don't know. A modest, honest finding beats a confident, wrong one.

**In real life:** If 8 of your 10 friends answered, that's 8 friends. It's not "users".

---

## Common mistakes

- Asking far too many questions
- Leading or loaded wording
- Running a survey before talking to anyone
- Drawing big conclusions from a handful of answers
