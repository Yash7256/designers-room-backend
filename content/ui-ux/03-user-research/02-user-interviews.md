# User interviews

*A good interview is mostly listening. Ask about real moments from the past, not opinions about the future.*

## TL;DR

A good interview is mostly listening. Ask about real moments from the past, not opinions about the future.

---

## Chapter 1: "Would you use it?" is a trap

You ask a friend, "Would you use an app for this?" They say, "Yeah, sounds cool!" You feel great. You've learned almost nothing.

People are kind, and they're bad at predicting their own behavior. A compliment isn't data. What you want are stories about what they actually did, and why.

| Weak question | Better question |
| --- | --- |
| Would you use an app for this? | Tell me about the last time you had to do this. |
| Don't you hate waiting? | How did you feel while you were waiting? |
| Do you like this feature? | What were you trying to do when you opened it? |

---

## Chapter 2: Five question habits

1. **Ask about the past.** "The last time you..." gets real stories. "Would you ever..." gets polite guesses.
2. **Ask open questions.** "What", "how" and "tell me about" invite stories. Yes or no questions end the conversation.
3. **Ask one thing at a time.** Two questions in one means they answer the easier one.
4. **Follow up.** "Why was that?" and "Tell me more" are two of the most useful phrases in research.
5. **Don't lead.** "Don't you think the checkout is confusing?" puts the answer in their mouth.

---

## Chapter 3: Run the interview

1. **Before:** Pick three to five people who match your user. Ask politely, and tell them how long it'll take. About 20 to 30 minutes is plenty for a first round.
2. **Before:** Write five to seven open questions. Treat them as a guide, not a script.
3. **At the start:** Explain what it's for, how you'll use their answers, and ask permission to take notes or record. Keep their name private. If they're under 18, ask a parent or guardian first.
4. **During:** Listen far more than you talk. Don't pitch your idea. Don't correct them. If there's a silence, wait. People often fill it with the most interesting thing they say.
5. **After:** Write up your notes within a day, while you still remember. Mark real quotes and real behaviors, not your interpretations.

**In real life:** You've probably told someone about a bad food delivery experience and they just let you talk. By the end you'd said things you hadn't planned to. That's what a good interviewer does for you.

---

## Chapter 4: Find patterns, not quotes

After three to five interviews, lay your notes side by side. Look for things that repeat: the same frustration, the same workaround, the same moment of worry.

One person's complaint is a story. Three people mentioning the same thing is a pattern. Group similar notes together, and name each group.

Stay honest about what this is. A handful of conversations won't prove something is true for everyone. It tells you where to look next and what to test.

---

## Common mistakes

- Pitching your idea instead of learning
- Leading questions
- Only asking friends who'll be nice
- Talking more than listening

## Try it (25 min)

- [ ] Pick a task, like "ordering food late at night".
- [ ] Write five open questions that start with "Tell me about the last time...".
- [ ] Interview one friend for about 15 minutes. Don't pitch anything.
- [ ] Write down three things that surprised you.
