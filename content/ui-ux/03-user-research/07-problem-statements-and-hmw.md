# Problem statements & HMW

*A problem statement says who needs what and why, in one sentence. "How might we" turns it into questions you can brainstorm on.*

## TL;DR

A problem statement says who needs what and why, in one sentence. "How might we" turns it into questions you can brainstorm on.

---

## Chapter 1: "Build an app" is not a problem

Someone says, "We need an app for hostel laundry." Sounds like a plan. But it's a solution, picked before anyone checked the problem.

The actual problem might be: "Students keep carrying their laundry down and finding the machine already taken." Describe it that way and suddenly other options appear: a booking sheet, a sign on the door, a simple message group. Maybe an app, maybe not.

**In real life:** A tiffin service that says "we need to build a mobile app" when what customers actually say is "I never know if the delivery has left yet." That might be fixed with one text message.

---

## Chapter 2: A simple template

Try this sentence shape:

**[User] needs [need] because [insight].**

For example: "First-year hostel students need to know when a washing machine is free because they keep walking down with a bucket of clothes and finding it taken."

| Not a problem statement | A better one |
| --- | --- |
| We need a laundry app. | First-year hostel students need to know when a washing machine is free because they keep carrying clothes down and finding it taken. |

Four rules: a specific user, a need and not a solution, an insight that comes from your research, and one sentence.

---

## Chapter 3: How might we

"How might we" (HMW) questions turn a problem into open invitations to brainstorm. They're phrased as possibilities, not decisions.

From the laundry problem, three HMWs at different sizes:

- **Too big:** How might we fix laundry? (Where would you even start?)
- **Too small:** How might we add a "free" button? (You've already picked the solution.)
- **Just right:** How might we help students know a machine is free before they leave their room?

1. Write five to ten HMWs. Don't judge yet.
2. Pick the two or three most promising.
3. Keep them handy. They'll feed straight into sketching in Stage 04.

---

## Chapter 4: Check it before you commit

Run your statement through four quick checks.

- [ ] Does it come from research, not just from a hunch?
- [ ] Could someone who wasn't in the room understand it?
- [ ] Does it avoid naming a solution?
- [ ] Would your user read it and say "yes, that's me"?

If any answer is no, rewrite it. And remember it can change. After you test your first ideas, come back and see if the problem still holds.

---

## Common mistakes

- Hiding a solution inside the problem
- Going too broad, like "improve education"
- Writing it with no research behind it
- Writing it once and never revisiting it
