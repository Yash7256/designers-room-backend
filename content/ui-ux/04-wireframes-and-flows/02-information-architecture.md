# Information architecture

*Information architecture is how you organize and name things so people can find them without thinking.*

## TL;DR

Information architecture is how you organize and name things so people can find them without thinking.

---

## Chapter 1: The kirana store test

Walk into a well-organized kirana store. Atta, dal and rice are near each other. Snacks have their own spot. You find what you need without asking. Walk into a badly organized one and you wander in circles.

Information architecture (IA) is the aisle layout of an app or website. Good IA is almost invisible. Bad IA makes people give up.

**In real life:** An app that has "Account", "Profile", "Settings" and "My Stuff", each holding a different piece of the same thing. Nobody knows where to look.

---

## Chapter 2: Four parts of IA

- **Organization:** How things are grouped together.
- **Labeling:** What each group and link is called.
- **Navigation:** How people move between things.
- **Search:** The backup for when browsing doesn't work.

---

## Chapter 3: Group and label with real people

You know your product too well. Your groups make sense to you because you built it. So test them with others.

1. **List everything.** Every feature and piece of content. This is an inventory.
2. **Write each on a card.** One thing per card.
3. **Try card sorting.** Give the cards to a few people and ask them to group them in ways that make sense to them.
4. **Choose open or closed.** In an open sort, people make up their own group names. In a closed sort, you give the group names and they place the cards.
5. **Use their words.** If people say "Orders" and your team says "Fulfilment", use "Orders".

A handful of people is enough to spot big disagreements. It's not enough to prove anything, so treat the result as a strong hint.

---

## Chapter 4: Draw a sitemap

A sitemap is a tree that shows every section and page, and how they connect. It's like a family tree for your product.

1. **Start with a handful of main sections.** The things people come for most.
2. **Place sub-pages beneath them.** Related things stay together.
3. **Keep important things close to the top.** If people have to dig deep, they stop.
4. **Check it with a task.** Ask a friend, "Where would you go to change your address?" and watch where they point.

The goal is clarity, not cleverness. Not too many choices at once, and not too many levels deep.

---

## Common mistakes

- Naming things in company jargon
- Burying important things deep
- Copying another app's menu without checking if it fits your users
- Too many top-level items

---

## Try it (25 min)

- [ ] Pick an app you use and list about fifteen features.
- [ ] Write each on a card and ask two or three friends to group them.
- [ ] Draw a simple sitemap from what you learn.
- [ ] Ask a friend, "Where would you find ...?" for three things.
