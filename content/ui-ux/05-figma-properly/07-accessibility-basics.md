# Accessibility basics

*Accessible design works for more people in more situations: tired eyes, bright sunlight, one hand, a screen reader, a keyboard. Build it in from the start.*

## TL;DR

## Chapter 1: A ramp beside the stairs

A ramp is built for wheelchairs. But look who else uses it: people with suitcases, parents with prams, someone with a sprained ankle. Something designed for one group ends up helping many.

Digital design works the same way.

**In real life:** Captions on videos. They were built for people who are deaf or hard of hearing. They're also used by people in noisy trains, quiet libraries, and anyone who just forgot their earphones.

---

## Chapter 2: Four ideas to remember

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) are organized around four principles, often shortened to POUR.

- **Perceivable:** Can people see, hear or otherwise sense the content?
- **Operable:** Can they use it with touch, mouse, keyboard or voice?
- **Understandable:** Is it clear and predictable?
- **Robust:** Does it work with assistive technology like screen readers?

---

## Chapter 3: A checklist for your designs

This isn't a full audit. It's the checks you can do in a design file before you hand anything over.

- [ ] Text contrast meets at least 4.5 to 1 for normal text (see Color & contrast).
- [ ] Color isn't the only way something is shown.
- [ ] Tap targets are big enough to hit.
- [ ] Form fields have visible labels, not just placeholder text that disappears.
- [ ] There's a clear focus state, so keyboard users can see where they are.
- [ ] Headings have a logical order.
- [ ] Meaningful images have text descriptions planned (alt text).
- [ ] Videos have captions.
- [ ] Nothing flashes, and motion is used sparingly.

---

## Chapter 4: Test in the real world

1. **Use your keyboard.** Press Tab through your design. Can you reach everything? Can you see where you are?
2. **Zoom the text.** Make it much bigger. Does the layout survive?
3. **Look in grayscale.** Does everything still make sense without color?
4. **Try a screen reader briefly.** VoiceOver on Apple devices, TalkBack on Android and NVDA on Windows are real options.
5. **Ask people.** Whenever you can, include people with disabilities in your research and testing.

This page covers the basics. Accessibility is a deep field, so use WCAG as your reference and bring in specialists when the stakes are high.

---

## Common mistakes

- Adding accessibility at the very end
- Using placeholder text as the only label
- Removing the focus outline because it "looks ugly"
- Assuming everyone sees and uses things the way you do
