# Visual hierarchy

*Decide what people see first, second and third.*

## TL;DR

Hierarchy is deciding what people see first, second and third, so they never have to guess.

---

## Chapter 1: The mom-yelling test

Think about how your mom calls you for dinner. "Hey." Ignorable. "Come eat." Fine, five minutes. Your full name, middle name included? Run.

Same person, same message, three levels of loud. That is hierarchy: you always know what is urgent without anyone explaining it.

Screens need the same clarity. If everything speaks at the same volume, the person has to decide what matters. That decision is work. Too much of it, and the screen feels tiring even when it looks fine.

### Loudness is relative

A single bold word on a quiet page feels important. A page where every word is bold has no important words.

This is the core tension of hierarchy. Emphasis is not a property of an element by itself. It is a relationship between elements. You make something important by making other things quieter.

### The dinner table, translated

| Order | Text | How loud |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st | Come eat. Now. | Big and bold |
| 2nd | Dinner's at 8, no rush | Medium |
| 3rd | P.S. there's dessert | Small and grey |

The person does not need a legend. The volume tells them what to do first.

### Hierarchy is not just size

Beginners often reach for size because it is obvious. Size is powerful, but it is only one knob. Weight, contrast, color, position and space all do work too.

The trick is not to turn every knob up. It is to choose the smallest number of knobs that makes the order obvious.

## Chapter 2: Your three volume knobs

Designers turn the "volume" of things up or down using a few tools. These do most of the work.

- **Size:** bigger gets noticed first.
- **Weight:** bolder gets noticed first.
- **Color and contrast:** stronger contrast, or a single accent color, pulls the eye.
- **Bonus, position and space:** in left-to-right languages, the top left is often read first. And a thing with lots of space around it feels important.

### Use one knob, not all of them

Here is the golden rule: turn one knob up on the thing that matters, and let the rest stay calm. Turn every knob up on everything and you are just yelling.

If the primary button is bigger, bolder, bright orange, underlined and surrounded by empty space, the other buttons disappear. That might be fine for one action, but it is exhausting if every action tries it.

### Contrast is a spotlight

Contrast can be loud or quiet. White text on a dark background is high contrast. Grey text on a dark background is lower contrast. A single accent color on a mostly neutral screen is a spotlight.

Use the spotlight where you want the eye to land. Do not put spotlights on the wallpaper.

### Position and space are quiet knobs

Position is cultural and habitual. In left-to-right languages, people often scan from the top left. Putting the most important thing there can help, but it is not a universal law.

Space is subtler. A thing with room around it feels important because nothing is competing with it. A cramped element feels like it is part of the crowd.

In real life: most chat apps. A chat with new messages gets a bold name and a badge. Quiet old chats fade back. Nobody taught you to read the list. It just works.

### Hierarchy changes with context

The same screen can have a different hierarchy on a marketing page and in a settings menu. On a marketing page, the headline might be the main thing. In settings, the toggle the person came to change is the main thing.

There is no universal order. There is the order that helps this person do this task right now.

## Chapter 3: The squint test

This is the easiest check in design, and it needs no tools. Squint at your screen until the text goes blurry, or zoom way out. What still stands out? Is it what should?

### How to run it

1. Squint at your screen. Or zoom out. Or apply a layer blur in Figma.
2. Name the first, second and third things you notice.
3. Compare that with what they should be. If the "Pay now" button is the fourth thing you see, that is a problem.
4. Adjust one knob at a time. Then squint again.

The squint test works because it removes detail. When you cannot read the words, you can still see the blocks, the contrast and the spacing. That is the structure the eye uses before it reads anything.

### What you are looking for

You want the visual order to match the task order. If the person needs to read the price before they pay, the price should be visible before the button. If the button is the main action, it should not be the last thing they find.

### Do not fix everything at once

If the squint test shows three problems, change one thing. Squint again. If you change five things, you will not know which change helped.

This is the same discipline as the spacing scale. Small, deliberate changes are easier to judge than a redesign.

## Chapter 4: One main thing per screen

Every screen has one thing the person most likely wants to do. Make that obvious. A screen with five "primary" buttons has no primary button at all.

### The three levels of action

A common approach is three levels of button:

- **Filled button** for the main action.
- **Outlined button** for a secondary action.
- **Plain text link** for the least important action.

"Pay now" gets filled. "Save for later" gets outlined. "Cancel" gets the text link. The person does not need to read every label to know which one matters.

### Primary does not mean biggest

The main action should be the easiest to find, not necessarily the largest thing on the screen. A giant button can overwhelm the content it is supposed to support.

Use size, weight and contrast together, but keep the rest of the screen calm. The main action wins because it is clearly different, not because everything else is shouting.

### One screen, one job

A screen can contain many tasks. That does not mean they all deserve the same volume.

Ask: if the person only does one thing here, what should it be? Design for that answer first. Then make the other actions available without competing.

If you cannot answer the question, the screen may be trying to do too much. That is a product problem wearing a design costume.

### Hierarchy is kindness

A clear hierarchy respects the person's attention. It says: here is what matters, here is what can wait, and here is where to go next.

That is why hierarchy is not about making things pretty. It is about making the next step obvious.

## Common mistakes

**Mistake 1: Making everything bold.** When everything is emphasized, nothing is. Bold only works if it is rare.

**Mistake 2: Five "primary" buttons on one screen.** If everything is primary, the person has to choose without guidance. Pick one main action.

**Mistake 3: Using color alone to say "this matters".** Color can help, but it is not enough. Size, weight, position and labels carry meaning too, including for people who cannot distinguish colors.

**Mistake 4: Hiding the key action in light grey.** Quiet is not the same as invisible. The main action can be understated, but it still has to be findable.

**Mistake 5: Trusting your eyes without the squint test.** You know the screen too well. Blur it and see what a tired person would see.

---

## Try it

Take a busy screen, yours or a screenshot of an app.

- [ ] Do the squint test and write down the first three things you notice.
- [ ] Decide what they should be.
- [ ] Fix it using only two knobs, size and weight, then squint again.

If the order still is not right, add contrast or space. Change one knob at a time and keep checking. The goal is not a dramatic redesign. The goal is an obvious next step.
