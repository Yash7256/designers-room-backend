# Typography basics

*Most of an interface is words. Make them easy to read.*

## TL;DR

Pick one or two typefaces, use a handful of sizes, and make text easy to read. Everything else is decoration.

---

## Chapter 1: Most of the interface is words

Open any app and count the pictures versus the words. Menus, buttons, prices, error messages, instructions, labels, empty states: it is mostly text.

So typography is not a finishing touch you add at the end. It is most of the design. If the type is hard to read, the screen is hard to use, no matter how good the layout looks.

Here is the idea to hold onto: type's first job is to be read. Making it pretty is the second job.

That order matters. A beautiful heading that is too light to read is a failed heading. A plain body style that people can read for ten minutes is a success.

In real life: a wedding card and a bus ticket use the same alphabet, but one sets a mood and the other does a job. Neither is wrong. Know which job your screen has.

### Type is part of the interface

Text is not a layer that sits on top of the design. The size, weight and spacing of words decide where the eye goes. A bold price and a small grey caption are already a hierarchy, even before you add color.

This is why typography and visual hierarchy are neighbours. You cannot separate "what it says" from "how it looks". The way it looks changes what people notice.

### Start with reading, not styling

Before you choose a font, ask three questions:

1. What does the person need to read first?
2. What do they need to read carefully?
3. What can stay small and quiet?

Those answers will do more for the screen than a font hunt.

## Chapter 2: Words designers use

Typography has its own vocabulary. You do not need all of it. These four terms will take you a long way.

- **Typeface:** the family of letter designs, like Inter or Georgia. People often say "font" for this too, and that is fine in everyday talk.
- **Weight:** how thick the strokes are, from thin to bold.
- **Line height:** the space from one line of text to the next. Too tight and lines crash into each other.
- **Serif vs sans-serif:** serif letters have little strokes on their ends, like Times New Roman. Sans-serif letters do not, like Arial. Both can be very readable.

### A few more terms you will hear

- **Type scale:** a small set of sizes used across the product.
- **Line length** or **measure:** how many characters fit on one line.
- **Tracking:** the space between all letters. Usually best left alone for body text.
- **Leading:** another word for line height, borrowed from print.

You do not have to memorise these to start. Learn the job each one does, then use the words when you need to explain a decision.

### One family is usually enough

A common beginner mistake is collecting typefaces like stickers. Five fonts on one screen do not make it more designed. They make it noisier.

One typeface in several weights can carry an entire product. If you add a second, give it a clear job, like headings only. The constraint helps.

## Chapter 3: Make it easy to read

Four quick checks. These are common guidelines, not laws, so use your eyes too.

1. **Size.** Body text on screens is commonly 16 pixels or larger. Tiny text quietly shuts people out.
2. **Line height.** Roomy lines help eyes find the next line. For body text, a line height of about 1.4 to 1.6 times the size is a common starting point.
3. **Line length.** Very long lines tire readers. A commonly cited comfortable range is roughly 45 to 75 characters per line.
4. **Contrast.** Enough difference between text and background. We cover this properly on the Color & contrast page.

### Size is not the same as hierarchy

Making the body text smaller to fit more on screen usually backfires. People lean in, zoom, or leave. If the content matters, give it a readable size.

At the same time, not every piece of text should be 16. A caption can be 12 to 14 if it is genuinely secondary and still has enough contrast. The question is not "what is the smallest size?" It is "what size does this text need to do its job?"

### Line height is a feeling

Tight line height feels dense and urgent. Loose line height feels calm and editorial. Most interfaces want something in between: enough room to breathe, not so much that the paragraph falls apart.

If you are unsure, start at 1.5 for body text and adjust. Then read a full paragraph, not just one line.

### Long lines make the eye work

When a line is too long, the eye has to travel further back to find the start of the next line. That small effort adds up over a page.

If your text container is very wide, do not make the font bigger to fill it. Instead, cap the measure. A column of text is often easier to read than a full-width paragraph.

### Accessibility is not a separate topic

Readable type is accessible type. People with low vision, tired eyes, small screens, bright sunlight or a language they are still learning all benefit from the same basics: enough size, enough contrast, enough line spacing.

You are not designing for an average person. You are designing for a range of people, in a range of conditions.

## Chapter 4: A tiny type scale

Instead of picking a new size every time, choose a small set and reuse it. A simple example looks like this. These numbers are just an example, not the answer.

1. **Title:** 32, bold. One per screen.
2. **Heading:** 24, medium. Starts a section.
3. **Subheading:** 18, medium. Starts a group.
4. **Body:** 16, regular. Most of your text.
5. **Caption:** 12 to 14, regular. Small notes and labels.

Use size and weight to separate levels, not ten different typefaces. One typeface in several weights is enough for most screens.

### Why five styles beat fifty

A type scale is a decision you make once. After that, every piece of text has a home. You stop asking "should this be 17 or 18?" and start asking "is this a heading or body?"

The screen calms down because the same level looks the same everywhere. The person reading does not have to relearn the visual language from page to page.

### Name the styles by job

Name your styles by what they do, not by what they look like: Title, Heading, Body, Caption. "Big bold orange" describes a style today and lies tomorrow. "Title" survives a palette change.

In real life: a good shop signboard has three sizes. The shop name is huge, "Since 1990" is small, and the phone number is readable from the road. Three sizes, one clear order.

## Common mistakes

**Mistake 1: Using five different typefaces.** More fonts rarely means more personality. It usually means less coherence.

**Mistake 2: Light grey tiny text that's hard to read.** Secondary text still has to be readable. If it is too faint, it is not quiet, it is gone.

**Mistake 3: ALL CAPS paragraphs.** All caps is fine for short labels. For longer text it slows reading down and feels like shouting.

**Mistake 4: Center-aligning long paragraphs.** Centered text is fine for a short title or a pull quote. For body copy, the uneven starting edge makes every line a small search.

**Mistake 5: Choosing a font before a job.** Start with readability and hierarchy. The typeface is the last decision, not the first.

## Try it

Take any screen you have made, or a screenshot of an app.

- [ ] Make three text styles in Figma: Title, Body and Caption.
- [ ] Apply them to every piece of text. No one-off sizes allowed.
- [ ] Notice how the screen calms down.

If a piece of text does not fit any of the three, decide whether it is a missing level or an exception. Most of the time, it is a missing level. Add it to the scale instead of inventing it inline.
