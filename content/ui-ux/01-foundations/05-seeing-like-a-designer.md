# Seeing like a designer

*How to notice what everyone else walks past.*

## TL;DR

Designers are not born with better eyes. They have a practice of noticing. They look at ordinary screens and ask: What is this trying to do? What is my eye drawn to? What choice made it feel this way? You can build that practice in a few minutes a day, starting with the apps you already use.

---

## Chapter 1: Attention is a design material

When you open a screen, your eye goes somewhere first. That is not an accident.

A designer chose:

- What is largest.
- What is brightest.
- What is closest to the top.
- What is surrounded by empty space.
- What moves.
- What is the only thing that looks like a button.

Your attention is the result of those choices.

### Notice the first thing

Before you judge a screen, notice where your eye lands.

Then ask:

- Why did it land there?
- Is that the most important thing on the screen?
- Is that what the designer wanted me to do?
- Is that what I actually needed?

Sometimes the answer is yes. Sometimes the design is pulling you toward a promotion while you are trying to find your order.

### Notice the second thing

The first thing is usually obvious. The second thing tells you more about hierarchy.

What did you see after the headline? Was it the price, the button, the fine print, or the empty space? The order of attention is the design's argument about what matters.

### Notice what is missing

Design is also what is not there.

- No clear next step.
- No error message.
- No confirmation.
- No way back.
- No explanation of why the app needs permission.

Missing elements are harder to see because there is nothing to look at. Train yourself to ask: what would I expect here that is not here?

## Chapter 2: Look for the choices, not the taste

Most people react to a screen with "I like it" or "I don't like it." A designer looks for the choices behind that reaction.

### The four questions

When you study a screen, ask:

1. **Hierarchy:** What is the most important thing, and how do I know?
2. **Spacing:** What is grouped together, and what is separated?
3. **Contrast:** What stands out, and what recedes?
4. **Consistency:** What repeats, and what breaks the pattern?

These are not the only questions. They are a reliable starting set.

### Hierarchy

Hierarchy is the order in which the eye reads the screen.

Look for:

- Size differences.
- Weight differences.
- Colour differences.
- Position.
- White space.

A screen with no hierarchy makes everything equally loud. A screen with too much hierarchy makes one thing scream and everything else whisper.

### Spacing

Spacing is how the design says "these belong together" or "these are separate."

Look for:

- The gap between a label and its input.
- The gap between sections.
- The margin around the main content.
- Whether the spacing is consistent.

If the gaps feel random, the screen will feel noisy even if the colours are nice.

### Contrast

Contrast is not just light versus dark. It is any difference that creates emphasis.

- Large versus small.
- Bold versus regular.
- Colour versus neutral.
- Filled versus outlined.
- Busy versus empty.

Contrast tells the eye what to care about. Too little contrast and everything blurs. Too much and nothing feels calm.

### Consistency

Consistency is a promise: similar things behave similarly.

Look for:

- Do all primary buttons look the same?
- Do all links look like links?
- Do all cards use the same spacing?
- Does the same action use the same word every time?

Inconsistency makes people hesitate. Hesitation is friction.

## Chapter 3: Read the intent, then question it

Every screen has an intent. Sometimes it is yours. Sometimes it is the business's. Sometimes it is an accident.

### Ask what the screen wants

A screen is usually trying to get you to do something:

- Buy.
- Sign up.
- Continue.
- Share.
- Stay.
- Trust.

That is not automatically bad. A product needs people to do things. But it is worth noticing when the screen's intent and your intent are different.

Example: a checkout that adds a newsletter subscription by default. The screen's intent is to grow the list. Your intent is to buy one thing. The design is working for someone, just not only you.

### Ask what the screen assumes

Screens assume things about you:

- That you know what "SSO" means.
- That you have a company email.
- That you are on a fast connection.
- That you read English.
- That you have used a similar app before.
- That you can see small grey text.

When you notice an assumption, you have found a potential accessibility or clarity problem.

### Ask what the screen is afraid of

Some designs are shaped by fear:

- Fear of support tickets, so every field has a long explanation.
- Fear of legal issues, so every page has a wall of terms.
- Fear of looking empty, so every screen is full of cards.
- Fear of users making mistakes, so every action needs a confirmation.

Fear is a real constraint. But when it drives the design, the person using it often pays the cost.

## Chapter 4: Build a noticing practice

You do not need a special app or a design degree. You need repetition.

### The five-minute teardown

Pick one screen you use daily. Set a timer for five minutes. Answer:

1. What is this screen for?
2. What did I notice first?
3. What did I notice second?
4. What is grouped together?
5. What is one thing I would change, and why?

Write it down. The writing is the practice. It forces you to turn a vague feeling into a specific observation.

### Keep a screenshot library

Screenshot screens that make you feel something:

- Delight.
- Confusion.
- Trust.
- Frustration.
- Calm.

Put them in one folder. Add a one-line note: why you saved it.

Over time, you will see patterns. You will also have evidence for your own work. When someone asks why a design feels off, you can point to a specific choice.

### Study outside your category

If you only look at apps like the one you are designing, you will copy the same assumptions.

Look at:

- Banking apps for trust and error states.
- Games for feedback and motivation.
- Maps for complexity and progressive disclosure.
- Streaming apps for browsing and choice overload.
- Government sites for accessibility and plain language.

Borrow the pattern, not the surface.

### Critique without cruelty

Noticing is not the same as complaining. The goal is to understand, not to feel superior.

A useful habit: describe what you see before you judge it.

- "The primary action is the same colour as the background."
- "The error message tells me what happened but not what to do."
- "The two headings are the same size, so I do not know which section I am in."

Description makes the critique specific. Specific critique can be acted on.

## Common mistakes

**Mistake 1: Looking only at the surface.** A pretty screen can still be confusing. Look for the decisions underneath.

**Mistake 2: Trusting your first reaction.** Your first reaction is data, not a verdict. Ask why you reacted that way.

**Mistake 3: Only studying good design.** Bad design teaches you what to avoid. Study both.

**Mistake 4: Collecting without reflecting.** A screenshot folder is not a practice. The note is the practice.

**Mistake 5: Confusing taste with evidence.** "I don't like it" is a starting point. "I can't find the next step" is a finding.

## Try it

Screenshot five screens you use daily.

For each one, write:

1. What is this screen for?
2. What did I notice first?
3. What is one choice that made me notice it?
4. What is one thing I would change?

Do this for a week. You will start seeing the choices before you see the surface.
