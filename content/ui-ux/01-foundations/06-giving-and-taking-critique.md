# Giving and taking critique

*How to make feedback useful instead of personal.*

## TL;DR

Critique is not a verdict on you. It is information about how the work lands for someone else. Good critique is specific, tied to the work, and focused on the effect rather than the person. Good receiving is curious, not defensive. The goal is not to agree with everything. The goal is to understand enough to make a better decision.

---

## Chapter 1: Critique is not criticism

The words sound similar. They are not the same.

Criticism often means judgement: "This is bad." Critique means examination: "Here is what I notice, here is what I wonder, here is what might happen."

### The purpose of critique

Critique exists to make the work better. That means:

- Surfacing what is working so it does not get lost.
- Finding what is confusing or broken.
- Testing the designer's assumptions.
- Giving the maker options they could not see alone.

It is not a performance. It is not a chance to prove you have better taste. It is a working conversation.

### The maker is not the work

A design is something you made. It is not you.

This sounds obvious. It does not feel obvious when someone says your layout is confusing. The useful move is to treat the feedback as information about the work, not a statement about your worth.

That is easier said than done. It gets easier with practice, and with critique that is actually useful.

### Why bad critique feels personal

Bad critique often sounds like:

- "I don't like it."
- "It feels off."
- "Can you make it pop?"
- "This is wrong."

These statements are about the critic's reaction, not the work. They give the maker nothing to act on. They also make the conversation about taste, which is unwinnable.

Good critique replaces the verdict with an observation.

## Chapter 2: Giving critique that people can use

The best critique is specific, kind, and actionable.

### Start with what you notice

Describe what you see before you judge it.

- "The primary button is the same colour as the secondary button."
- "The heading is much larger than the body, so my eye goes there first."
- "The error message says 'Invalid input' but does not say which field."

This gives the maker a shared observation. It also keeps you honest: if you cannot describe it, you may be reacting to something else.

### Separate observation from interpretation

Observation: "The two sections have the same visual weight."

Interpretation: "I am not sure which one I should read first."

Both are useful. The first is evidence. The second is the effect. Say both, but do not confuse them.

### Use the "I notice / I wonder / What if" format

This is a simple structure that keeps critique open.

- **I notice:** "I notice the save button is below the fold."
- **I wonder:** "I wonder if people will think the form is finished before they see it."
- **What if:** "What if the save action stayed visible while scrolling?"

The format works because it:
- Starts with evidence.
- Names the uncertainty.
- Offers a direction without prescribing a solution.

### Ask before you advise

Sometimes the most useful question is: "What are you trying to do here?"

You may be critiquing a choice that is deliberate. You may be missing a constraint. Ask first. Then respond to the actual problem.

### Critique the work, not the person

Avoid:

- "You always make things too busy."
- "This is a mess."
- "You should know better."

Use:

- "This section feels busy to me. I am not sure where to look first."
- "The spacing here makes me read these as one group. Was that intentional?"
- "I am missing the next step. What do you want me to do after this?"

The second set is about the work. The first set is about the maker.

### Say what is working

Critique is not only a list of problems.

- "The hierarchy here is clear. I know what to read first."
- "The empty state is reassuring. I know what to do next."
- "The error message tells me how to fix it. That is rare."

Naming what works helps the maker keep it. It also makes the critique feel balanced without being fake.

## Chapter 3: Receiving critique without shutting down

Receiving critique is a skill. It is not passive. You are actively listening, sorting, and deciding.

### Listen first, defend later

The first instinct is often to explain why you did it. Resist that for a moment.

The person is telling you how the work landed for them. That is data. You can disagree with their interpretation later. First, understand it.

### Ask clarifying questions

Useful questions:

- "What did you notice first?"
- "Where did you get stuck?"
- "What did you expect to happen?"
- "Is this about the flow or the visual?"
- "What would make this clearer for you?"

These questions turn a vague reaction into something specific.

### Separate the feedback from the solution

Someone might say: "Make the button bigger."

The useful question is: "What problem would that solve for you?"

Maybe the button is hard to find. Maybe the label is unclear. Maybe the page is too busy. The solution they suggest may not be the best one, but the problem is real.

### You do not have to agree with everything

Listening does not mean obeying.

You are allowed to say:

- "That is a real problem. I am not sure a bigger button is the fix."
- "I see what you mean. That was a deliberate trade-off because of X."
- "I want to test that before changing it."

The goal is a better decision, not unanimous approval.

### Notice your defensiveness

Defensiveness is normal. It shows up as:

- Explaining before listening.
- Focusing on why the critic is wrong.
- Changing the subject.
- Taking the feedback as a personal attack.

When you notice it, pause. Ask one more question. Buy yourself a moment to think.

### Take notes, not orders

Write down the feedback. You do not have to act on it immediately.

Later, sort it:

- Fix now: blocks the main task.
- Test: unclear if it is a real problem.
- Consider: valid, but not for this version.
- Decline: based on a misunderstanding or a different goal.

This turns critique into a decision instead of a mood.

## Chapter 4: Building a critique habit

Critique works best when it is regular, small, and safe.

### Make it specific

"Do you like it?" is not a useful question. Ask:

- "What do you think this screen is for?"
- "What would you do first?"
- "What is confusing?"
- "Where would you stop?"

Specific questions get specific answers.

### Make it early

Critique is most useful before you are attached to the work.

Show a rough sketch. Show a flow. Show a wireframe. The earlier the feedback, the cheaper it is to change.

### Make it safe

People will not share real reactions if they are afraid of being judged. A few norms help:

- Critique the work, not the person.
- Ask before advising.
- Say what is working.
- Do not pile on.
- Do not defend the work for the maker.

### Make it reciprocal

If you want useful critique, give useful critique. The habit spreads.

When someone shows you their work, use the same format you want them to use with yours: I notice, I wonder, what if.

### Critique your own work

You can run the practice alone.

- Show your screen to someone who has never seen it.
- Ask them what they think it is for.
- Watch where they hesitate.
- Write down one thing you would change.

You do not need a formal review to learn from critique.

---

## Common mistakes

**Mistake 1: Confusing critique with taste.** "I don't like it" is not a critique. It is a reaction. Turn it into an observation.

**Mistake 2: Defending before listening.** You cannot learn from feedback you are arguing with in your head.

**Mistake 3: Only saying what is wrong.** A critique that ignores what works is not balanced. It is just a list of problems.

**Mistake 4: Changing everything at once.** Not all feedback is equally important. Sort it before you act.

**Mistake 5: Waiting until the work is polished.** Late critique is expensive. Early critique is cheap.

## Try it

Take one screen you made.

Write three notes:

1. **I notice:** one thing that is working, and one thing that is not.
2. **I wonder:** one question about how someone else would experience it.
3. **What if:** one change you could test, not necessarily ship.

Then ask someone else to do the same. Compare your notes. The gap between them is the interesting part.
