# UX vs UI vs Product

*Same job, different job titles, and why the difference actually matters.*

## TL;DR

UX design is about how something works and feels over time. UI design is about the surface: layout, colour, type, spacing, motion. Product design is the widest of the three: it asks what should exist, for whom, and why.

In small teams, one person often does all three. In larger teams, they are separate roles. The labels matter less than the decisions you are responsible for.

---

## Chapter 1: UX is the experience, not the screen

UX stands for user experience. The word "experience" is doing a lot of work.

A user's experience includes:

- What they expected before they opened the product.
- Whether they could find what they needed.
- How long it took.
- How it felt when something went wrong.
- Whether they trusted it enough to come back.

Most of that is not on the screen. It is in the flow, the copy, the timing, the error states, and the support email they had to send.

### UX is a sequence, not a snapshot

A UI designer can make one screen beautiful. A UX designer has to care about what happens before and after that screen.

Example: a password reset flow.

- The UI might be a clean form with a clear button.
- The UX asks: How did the person get here? Do they know which email they used? What happens if the email never arrives? Can they recover without contacting support? How long does the link last? What if they click it on a different device?

The UI is one frame. The UX is the whole story.

### UX is often invisible

Good UX is quiet. The person just does the thing. Bad UX is loud: they get stuck, they blame themselves, they leave.

This is why UX work can be hard to show in a portfolio. A beautiful screen is easy to screenshot. A flow that prevented twelve confusing moments is not.

## Chapter 2: UI is the surface that carries the experience

UI stands for user interface. It is the part the person sees and touches.

That includes:

- Layout and spacing.
- Typography.
- Colour and contrast.
- Icons and imagery.
- Components: buttons, inputs, cards, modals.
- Motion and feedback.
- Responsive behaviour.

UI is not "just visual." It is how the experience becomes legible.

### A UI decision is still a UX decision

Choosing a 14px label instead of a 12px label is a UI decision. It is also a UX decision, because it changes whether someone can read the label.

The categories overlap. That is normal.

The useful distinction is not "UX is thinking, UI is drawing." It is:

- UX owns the logic and the journey.
- UI owns the surface and the moment.

### Why UI gets mistaken for the whole job

UI is the most visible part. It is what people screenshot, critique, and remember. It is also the easiest part to show in a portfolio.

But a beautiful interface on top of a broken flow is a beautiful dead end.

## Chapter 3: Product design is the wider question

Product design sits above both. It asks:

- Should we build this at all?
- Who is it for?
- What problem does it solve?
- How will we know it worked?
- What should we not build?

A product designer still cares about UX and UI. They just also care about the business, the roadmap, and the trade-offs between them.

### The three roles in one sentence

- **UX designer:** How does this work for the person?
- **UI designer:** How does this look and feel in the moment?
- **Product designer:** What should this be, and why?

### Where the roles blur

At a startup, the product designer might do research, flows, UI, prototyping, and QA. At a large company, the UX researcher might own interviews, the interaction designer might own flows, the UI designer might own the visual system, and the product manager might own the business case.

Job titles are not standards. They are local agreements.

### What matters more than the title

When you are learning, do not worry about which box you fit into. Worry about which decisions you can make well.

Can you explain why a flow works? Can you make a screen clear? Can you ask a useful question about the business? Those skills travel.

## Chapter 4: What each role actually does day to day

### A UX designer's week

- Talks to users or reads research.
- Maps flows and edge cases.
- Writes problem statements and hypotheses.
- Sketches and tests low-fidelity ideas.
- Works with engineers on feasibility.
- Reviews the built experience and files the gaps.

### A UI designer's week

- Explores layout and visual direction.
- Builds components and states.
- Refines type, spacing, colour, and motion.
- Makes sure the design system is consistent.
- Prepares files for handoff.
- Checks the implementation against the design.

### A product designer's week

- Frames the problem with product and engineering.
- Decides what to learn before building.
- Designs the flow and the surface.
- Facilitates trade-off conversations.
- Measures the outcome after launch.
- Decides what to do next.

### The overlap is the point

The best teams do not defend their borders. They share the problem. The UX designer learns enough about visual craft to communicate. The UI designer learns enough about flows to spot a dead end. The product designer learns enough about both to make a coherent call.

## Common mistakes

**Mistake 1: Thinking UX is only research.** Research is part of UX. So is the flow, the copy, the error state, and the follow-up email.

**Mistake 2: Thinking UI is only colour.** UI is hierarchy, spacing, type, states, responsiveness, and feedback. Colour is one tool.

**Mistake 3: Treating product design as "UX plus business."** Product design is not a promotion. It is a different scope of responsibility.

**Mistake 4: Fighting over titles.** Titles help you get hired. They do not make the work better.

**Mistake 5: Skipping the overlap.** If you only do one part, learn enough about the others to avoid handing over a problem you could have caught.

## Try it

Open one app you use daily. Write down:

1. Three UX decisions it contains.
2. Three UI decisions it contains.
3. One product decision it contains.

Then ask: which of those decisions would you have missed if you only looked at the screen?
