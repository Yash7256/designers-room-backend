# What design actually is

*A friendly first look at the job behind the job.*

## TL;DR

Design is the practice of making deliberate choices so that something works better for a person. It is not decoration added at the end. It is not "make it pop." It is the work of noticing a problem, understanding who has it, and arranging an outcome so the problem is smaller.

You already think like a designer more often than you realise. Rearranging your desk so you stop knocking over your coffee? Design. Choosing a route to the station that avoids the busy crossing? Design. The difference between that and professional design is that a designer does it on purpose, for other people, and can explain why.

---

## Chapter 1: Design is a decision, not a decoration

Most people meet design as a surface. A logo. A colour. A font. Something added to make a thing look nicer.

That is the smallest possible part of it.

Design is a decision-making discipline. Every design decision answers a question:

- Should this button be here, or lower down?
- Should this screen say three things, or one?
- Should this flow ask for payment before or after the account is created?
- Should this error message tell the person what went wrong, or what to do next?

The visual part matters. But the visual part is how the decision becomes usable. The decision comes first.

### The coffee cup test

Imagine two coffee cups.

Cup A is beautiful. The handle is too small for an average hand. The lid leaks if you tilt it more than ten degrees. The cup is hot to hold. It photographs perfectly.

Cup B is plain. The handle fits. The lid seals. The cup stays warm without burning your hand. It looks like a cup.

Cup A wins the photo. Cup B wins breakfast.

Design is the work of making Cup B, and then making it look like it was worth buying.

### Why this matters for you

If you think design is decoration, you will judge your own work by whether it looks impressive. You will spend hours choosing a colour and ten minutes deciding what the screen is for.

If you think design is decisions, you will ask better questions earlier. You will still care about how it looks, but you will know why.

## Chapter 2: The designer's real job: making choices on purpose

A designer's job is not to have taste. It is to make choices that hold up when someone asks why.

That sounds simple. It is not, because most design choices involve trade-offs.

### Trade-offs are the job

Every product has constraints: time, money, technical limits, business goals, legal requirements, and the fact that people are busy and easily confused. Design lives inside those constraints.

A few examples:

- A checkout flow could be one screen or five. One screen is faster if everything goes right. Five screens are clearer when something goes wrong.
- A settings page could show every option. That is honest, but overwhelming. Hiding options is cleaner, but can make people feel controlled.
- A mobile app could use a bottom navigation bar. It is easy to reach, but it takes up space on every screen.

There is no universally correct answer. There is a correct answer for a specific person, in a specific context, with specific constraints.

### The three questions behind most design work

When a designer is doing the job well, they are usually answering:

1. **Who is this for?** Not "everyone." A specific person with a specific situation.
2. **What are they trying to do?** The task, not the feature.
3. **What is in the way?** The friction, confusion, fear, or missing information.

Everything else – layout, colour, type, motion – is in service of those three questions.

### Design is not the same as art

Art can be ambiguous on purpose. Design usually cannot.

If a painting makes you feel something without knowing why, that is a success. If a sign makes you feel something without knowing where the toilet is, that is a failure.

This does not mean design cannot be beautiful or emotional. It means beauty and emotion are tools, not the whole brief.

## Chapter 3: Why "good design" is not the same as "design I like"

Taste is personal. Design quality is not entirely personal.

You can dislike a design and still recognise that it works. You can love a design and still recognise that it is confusing.

### The difference between preference and evidence

"I don't like orange" is a preference. It is real, but it is not an argument about whether the design works.

"The contrast between the orange text and the background is too low for people with low vision" is evidence. It points to a measurable problem and a specific group of people.

Good designers have preferences. They just do not confuse them with proof.

### What "good" usually means in practice

Good design tends to be:

- **Clear** – the person can tell what this is and what to do next.
- **Forgiving** – mistakes are easy to recover from.
- **Consistent** – similar things behave similarly.
- **Appropriate** – it fits the context and the audience.
- **Efficient** – it does not waste the person's time or attention.

Notice that "beautiful" is not on that list. Beauty is welcome. It is not the whole definition.

### A useful test

Show the design to someone who has never seen it. Ask them:

- What do you think this screen is for?
- What would you do first?
- What happens if you tap this?

If they can answer without you explaining, the design is doing its job. If they cannot, the problem is probably not the colour.

## Chapter 4: Where designers actually work

Design is not one job. It is a family of jobs, and the work looks different depending on where you sit.

### In a product team

You work with engineers, product managers, researchers, and sometimes data analysts. Your job is to turn a fuzzy problem into something a team can build and test. You will spend a lot of time in Figma, but you will spend more time in conversations.

### In an agency

You work on several clients at once. The pace is faster, the context changes often, and you need to get up to speed on a new industry quickly. You may have less access to real users, so you learn to ask sharper questions.

### In-house at a larger company

You may specialise. One designer owns the design system. Another owns onboarding. Another owns accessibility. The work is deeper, but the scope is narrower.

### Freelance

You are the designer, the project manager, the salesperson, and sometimes the therapist. Freedom is high. Stability is lower. You learn to scope work and say no.

### What they all share

Every design job is ultimately about making something clearer for someone else. The tools change. The job title changes. The core loop does not.

## Common mistakes

**Mistake 1: Starting with the tool.** Opening Figma before you understand the problem feels productive. It is usually procrastination with a cursor.

**Mistake 2: Designing for yourself.** You are not the user. Your habits, knowledge, and patience are not representative. Talk to someone else.

**Mistake 3: Treating "clean" as a goal.** Clean is a side effect of clarity. A clean screen that hides the next step is not good design.

**Mistake 4: Skipping the constraints.** A design that ignores engineering, legal, or business limits is not a design. It is a wish.

**Mistake 5: Defending instead of listening.** When someone says they are confused, the useful response is curiosity, not a lecture.

## Try it

Open an app you use every day. Pick one screen.

Write down:

1. What is this screen for?
2. What is the first thing your eye goes to?
3. What is the one action the screen wants you to take?
4. What is one thing that makes that action harder than it needs to be?

That is a design teardown. You just did the job, at a small scale.
